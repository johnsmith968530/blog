# The Least-Represented Psychological Theories

*An analysis of which modern psychological frameworks are conspicuous by their absence in the five stories of "We'll Prescribe You a Cat."*

The book is a useful test case for the "hodgepodge" quality of modern psychology: it's deliberately eclectic in what it *includes*, but it has real *gaps*, and the gaps are more revealing than the presences. The absences cluster in a telling way — and they're not random. They correspond to the theories that locate the therapeutic mechanism in **technique, protocol, or biology**, i.e., in something *the clinician does to or delivers to the patient*. What survives is the strand that locates the mechanism in **relationship and lived encounter**.

Here are the least-represented major theories, ranked roughly by how conspicuous the absence is given the material.

---

## 1. The biological / neurochemical model — actively rejected

This is the loudest absence, and it's not neutral: the book *refuses* it. Modern psychiatry's dominant frame — depression as serotonin dysregulation, insomnia as a GABA/melatonin problem, anxiolytics and SSRIs — is essentially walked offstage. Shuta's pre-clinic experience ("several psychiatrists… they would prescribe him similar medication… it wasn't the doctors, but the sleeping pills that helped") is framed as a dead end. The clinic itself is at pains to insist it's "not a psychiatric clinic" and prescribes a *cat* instead of a molecule. Given that pharmacotherapy is what most real patients actually receive, this isn't just an omission — it's a thesis statement. The biological layer is the one frame the author most wants out of the room.

## 2. Classical CBT (cognitive restructuring)

CBT is the most evidence-supported *psychotherapy* on offer, and it's nearly invisible. There are no thought records, no identification of cognitive distortions, no behavioral experiments in the technical sense, no Socratic challenging. Koga's distortions about Hinako aren't *disputed* — they're dissolved by lived experience (sleeping on a train, his daughter's lecture on the energy cost of praise, holding a cat). Tomoka's perfectionism isn't reframed cognitively; it's broken by a cat tearing her apartment apart. Where CBT would intervene at the level of *belief content*, Ishida intervenes at the level of *embodied encounter*. The one CBT-adjacent element is behavioral activation (Shuta's physical labor restoring sleep and appetite), but even that's incidental to the construction-job plot, not a protocol.

## 3. Psychodynamic / psychoanalytic — refused, despite the setup

This one is interesting because the book keeps *almost* becoming psychodynamic and then refuses. The doctor is a mysterious interpretive figure in an uncanny space — he looks like the perfect vessel for transference, for reading the patient's unconscious. But he does the opposite: he's glib, opaque, gives no interpretations, changes the subject, naps, gets drunk on catnip tea. He pointedly *won't* be analyzed through. There's no working-through, no exploration of early-childhood dynamics as causal (Story 3's childhood memory functions as attachment loss, not as Oedipal/developmental conflict). The clinic is almost an anti-analytic space — it withholds exactly the interpretive labor that defines the analytic stance. The refusal is itself a position.

## 4. Trauma-informed / PTSD frameworks

Striking, because the *raw material* is everywhere. The cats themselves are trauma survivors: B's siblings starved to death; Chitose was bred in an illegal mill; Mimita and Nike were rescued from a death-house unit. Abino's grief and Emoto's bullying both have trauma-shaped edges. Yet none of it is processed through a trauma lens — no stabilization, no narrative exposure, no grounding, no "the body keeps the score" somatic work, no PTSD language at all. The human suffering is treated as a *relationship deficit*, not as an unprocessed threat memory. Given how saturated modern mental-health discourse is in trauma language, the book's decision to route everything through attachment-and-loss instead is a real editorial choice, not an oversight.

## 5. Third-wave CBT (ACT, mindfulness, DBT)

Minimal. ACT's pillars — acceptance, cognitive defusion, values, committed action — are *thematically* adjacent (Story 5 is literally about acceptance and letting go; Shuta commits to valued action), but none of it is operationalized as practice. No mindfulness exercises, no values-clarification worksheets, no distress-tolerance skills, no "defusion" technique. The acceptance in Story 5 arrives through a mystical encounter with a nurse-who-is-also-the-cat, not through a defusion exercise. The themes are there; the technology isn't.

## 6. Positive psychology (Seligman)

Essentially absent, and tellingly so. No strengths inventories, no gratitude practice, no "flourishing" metrics, no savoring. The model is deficit-and-repair, not optimize-and-amplify. Even the "cured" patients aren't made happier in a positive-psychology sense — Shuta just sleeps and eats; Koga stops having nightmares; Abino learns to grieve. The book has no interest in well-being *above baseline*, only in restoring the capacity to connect.

---

## Honorable mentions (present enough to not be conspicuous absences)

- **Self-determination theory** (autonomy / competence / relatedness) — implicitly served by Shuta's construction work and found community, though never named.
- **Systems / family therapy** — Story 3 is intergenerational and Story 2 is family-dynamic, but there are no family sessions, genograms, or systemic interventions; the cat is the intervention, not the system.
- **Narrative therapy** — thematically latent (stories being re-told, the "perhaps they might even return" re-authoring) but not practiced.

---

## The pattern under the pattern

If you step back, the absent theories share a trait: **they locate the therapeutic mechanism in technique, protocol, or biology** — i.e., in something *the clinician does to or delivers to the patient*. What survives in the book is the strand of psychology that locates the mechanism in **relationship and lived encounter** — attachment, loss, reciprocity, embodied presence. The clinic withholds interpretation, medication, skills, and exposure alike; it only *provides a living other* and waits. So the absences aren't random hodgepodge-omissions — they're consistent with a worldview where healing is something that happens *between* beings, not something administered to one. The book is, in effect, an argument for which half of psychology's hodgepodge the author thinks is real.

## Reference: where each present theory actually lives

For contrast, here's the companion map of the frameworks the book *does* use, by story:

| Story | Patient | Dominant mechanism | Attachment fit |
|-------|---------|---------------------|----------------|
| 1. B | Shuta | Catalytic disruption + behavioral activation + found-family attachment | Present but downstream |
| 2. Margo | Koga | Affective reciprocity / breaking dismissiveness | Adjacent, not core |
| 3. Koyuki | Megumi & Aoba | Generational attachment disruption & repair | Strongest match (Bowlby) |
| 4. Tank and Tangerine | Tomoka | Loosening of control / tolerating imperfection | Weakest fit |
| 5. Mimita | Abino | Attachment-and-loss (grief work, letting go) | Strong match (Bowlby's *Loss*) |

The two strongest attachment stories (3 and 5) are the ones that map almost verbatim onto Bowlby's *Attachment and Loss*; the other three borrow attachment's vocabulary while running on a different engine.
